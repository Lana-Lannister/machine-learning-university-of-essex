Over twelve weeks, this module changed how I understand both machine learning and my responsibilities as a practitioner. I began by focusing on implementing algorithms and interpreting their outputs. By the end, I was asking whether those outputs were reliable, whose experiences they represented, and how clearly I could justify my decisions to others. Using the "What? So what? Now what?" framework outlined by Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001), this reflection examines my technical learning, contribution to team activities, and professional and personal development.

## What? From implementing algorithms to evaluating evidence

The early units introduced a range of techniques: Pearson correlation, regression, Jaccard distance for patient similarity, perceptrons and multi-layer perceptrons, convolutional neural networks applied to CIFAR-10, and gradient descent for minimising a cost function. These activities helped me connect the mechanisms of algorithms to their assumptions and applications. For example, comparing perceptrons with multi-layer networks developed my understanding of why model architecture matters when relationships are not linearly separable.

My perspective developed further during the model evaluation unit. Comparing AUC and R2 under different regularisation strengths and test-set sizes showed me that a performance score must be interpreted alongside the evaluation procedure. I began to move from asking "Does this model work?" to "What evidence would reveal its limitations?"

This shift became tangible in the Unit 6 group project. Group 1 used K-Means and K-Prototypes to segment Airbnb listings in New York City into three host personas, then compared global and cluster-specific Linear Regression and Random Forest models for price prediction. My contribution centred on running and validating the clustering and developing the regression experiments.

The results challenged our initial expectation: a single global model outperformed the cluster-specific alternatives. This taught me to distinguish between segmentation that supports interpretation and segmentation that improves prediction. The existence of meaningful clusters did not, by itself, justify training separate predictive models. Reporting the result also required us to accept a less convenient conclusion rather than select a configuration that supported our original hypothesis.

In the Unit 11 individual project, I compared SVD-GBDT with Hybrid NeuMF on MovieLens. I extended the evaluation beyond headline RMSE by including a Wilcoxon test, subgroup analysis by gender and activity level, and a probe investigating whether gender could be recovered from model representations. The small RMSE difference, approximately 0.003 stars, was not statistically significant under the test used. Meanwhile, the privacy probe suggested that omitting gender as an input did not prevent the representations from retaining information associated with it. Including these checks independently demonstrated a growing willingness to question model performance rather than simply report it.

## So what? Learning to exercise technical and collaborative judgement

My most significant technical lesson was that predictive accuracy alone provides an incomplete basis for model selection. Although the recommender models had similar RMSE results, they differed in catalogue coverage, popularity amplification, and subgroup fairness. These differences changed how I interpreted their relative strengths. A model could perform well on average while offering a narrower range of recommendations or serving some users less effectively.

I also became more careful about the conclusions that evaluation can support. A non-significant result does not establish that two models are equivalent; it means that the analysis did not provide sufficient evidence of a difference. Similarly, recoverable gender information raises concerns about attribute leakage, but does not alone demonstrate a particular downstream harm. This distinction matters because responsible evaluation requires restraint in interpreting findings as well as thoroughness in producing them.

The group project developed a different aspect of my practice. Working across time zones meant that modelling decisions had to remain understandable when I was unavailable to explain them. Cramton's (2001) discussion of the mutual knowledge problem helped me interpret this experience: information that seemed obvious within my own working context was not necessarily available to my teammates.

Initially, my documentation focused more on what I had done than why I had done it. This made handoffs less effective because colleagues had to reconstruct the reasoning behind my choices. I responded by recording the rationale for modelling decisions alongside the results. I learned that technical contribution includes making work usable by others, rather than treating communication as an administrative task after the analysis.

Working independently on Unit 11 gave me greater freedom to investigate significance, fairness, and privacy without coordinating each decision. However, it also exposed a weakness: without an immediate audience, my documentation became less consistent. This suggested that my documentation habits were still partly dependent on external accountability. Independence helped me explore more deeply, but it did not remove the need to make my reasoning explicit and reproducible.

Personally, these experiences increased my confidence in handling unexpected findings. I became more comfortable acknowledging that a plausible hypothesis might not be supported and that a technically successful model could still have important limitations.

## Now what? Turning reflection into consistent practice

I will carry forward a broader definition of model readiness. Successful training is a milestone, but judging whether a model is suitable for use requires evidence about its performance, limitations, and intended context.

In future projects, I will define an evaluation plan before comparing models. Alongside appropriate predictive metrics, this will include uncertainty or significance analysis where justified, examination of relevant subgroups, and measures connected to the application's purpose. For recommender systems, my experience shows the value of considering coverage and popularity effects alongside prediction error.

I will also examine potential attribute leakage where sensitive information could be inferred from model representations. The Unit 11 findings challenged my assumption that excluding a protected attribute was sufficient to address the issue. Future assessments will therefore consider what a model can encode, as well as which fields it explicitly receives.

To improve collaboration and reproducibility, I will maintain a decision log in both team and individual work. For each substantial modelling choice, I will record its rationale, the alternatives considered, and the evidence supporting the decision. Applying this consistently in individual projects will help me develop documentation as a sustained professional habit.

This module has strengthened my ability to contribute modelling expertise while remaining open to evidence that challenges my expectations. My next step is to make that critical approach consistent: testing assumptions, communicating uncertainty, and documenting decisions clearly enough for others, and my future self, to evaluate and continue the work.
