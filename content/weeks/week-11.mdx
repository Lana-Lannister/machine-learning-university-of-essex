## Assessment 1: Discussion Forum Summary Post

### Summary Post

_by Lana Menezes Fidelis, Thursday, 1 October 2026, 2:53 PM_

Looking back on this discussion, the main change in my thinking is the question I ask. I no longer ask whether AI writers are beneficial. I ask where uncertainty about their outputs can be tolerated.

My initial post focused on documented harms: fluent but ungrounded text, dangerous advice, radicalising content, and memorised personal data (Hutson, 2021; Bender et al., 2021). Dzemal's response exposed a gap in that argument. I had treated risk as a property of the model alone, when it depends on the model and the context it is used in. The evidence on benefits supports his reading. Noy and Zhang (2023) found that ChatGPT cut the time spent on professional writing tasks by 40% while improving quality. Doshi and Hauser (2024) found that AI-generated ideas made individual stories more creative but made the stories as a group less diverse. Neither finding removes the failures I described. Both benefits appear where an error is cheap to spot.

Patrick complicated the picture from the other side. The mitigations proposed by McGuffie and Newhouse (2020) and Carlini et al. (2021) are real, but their own authors describe them as partial. His question, whether the constraint is technical or institutional, made me reconsider the recent units material. A network's behaviour is learned from its training data rather than written by hand, so many risks cannot be fully tested away after training. That places responsibility upstream with developers, who have weak incentives to audit their systems without outside accountability (Raji et al., 2020). The EU AI Act's transparency obligations are one attempt to change those incentives, though how well they work has yet to be shown (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024).

I therefore no longer hold that the benefits are simply oversold. A more defensible conclusion is that the benefits are immediate and measurable, while the harms are spread out, delayed, and harder to trace to their source (Weidinger et al., 2021). That imbalance, more than capability itself, is what makes AI writers hard to govern.

## Assessment 2: Model Performance Measurement (AUC and R2)

### Assessment brief

Run the `model_Performance_Measurement.ipynb` file and change different parameters and observe its impact on AUC and R2 error.

**Learning outcome**

- Articulate the legal, social, ethical and professional issues faced by machine learning professionals.

### Walkthrough

#### What the notebook covers

The notebook is a reference tour of `scikit-learn`'s evaluation metrics rather than a single end-to-end model: confusion matrices, F1/accuracy/precision/recall (binary, multiclass, and multilabel), log loss, ROC AUC (binary, via the Breast Cancer dataset, and multiclass, via a one-vs-rest SVM on Iris), and the regression metrics MSE, MAE, and R2 on a small four-point toy example. Running it as provided surfaces a real bug: the cell that imports `accuracy_score` begins with a leading space (` from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score`), which raises an `IndentationError` in a fresh kernel, since a module-level import cannot be indented. The fix is simply to remove the leading space.

The notebook's own AUC example also has a methodological issue worth flagging before experimenting further: it fits `LogisticRegression` on the full Breast Cancer dataset and then computes AUC on that exact same data, with no held-out test set. That returned an AUC of **0.9946**, which is an optimistic, inflated number: it measures how well the model fits data it has already seen, not how well it would generalise to new patients. This is corrected in the experiments below by introducing a proper train/test split before computing AUC and R2.

#### Experiment 1: AUC vs. regularization strength (C), with a real held-out test set

Using the Breast Cancer dataset with a 70/30 train/test split, varying `LogisticRegression`'s inverse regularization strength `C`:

| C | Train AUC | Test AUC |
|-----|-----------|----------|
| 0.01 | 0.9850 | 0.9787 |
| 0.1 | 0.9916 | 0.9873 |
| 1.0 | 0.9953 | 0.9912 |
| 10.0 | 0.9961 | 0.9904 |
| 100.0 | 0.9966 | 0.9883 |

Train AUC keeps climbing as `C` increases (less regularization, the model fits the training data more closely), but test AUC peaks around `C = 1.0` and then declines at `C = 10` and `C = 100`. This is overfitting made visible in a single metric: beyond a certain point, extra flexibility improves the fit to training data while making generalisation to unseen data slightly worse.

#### Experiment 2: AUC vs. test set size

Still on Breast Cancer, fixing `C = 1.0` and varying the held-out proportion:

| test_size | n_test | Test AUC |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| 0.1 | 57 | 0.9974 |
| 0.2 | 114 | 0.9940 |
| 0.3 | 171 | 0.9912 |
| 0.5 | 285 | 0.9928 |
| 0.7 | 399 | 0.9880 |

The very small test set (`test_size = 0.1`, only 57 patients) produces the highest AUC of the whole sweep, not because the model is actually better, but because a smaller sample gives a noisier, less reliable estimate that happened to land high here. Larger, more stable test sets converge toward a more trustworthy figure in the low-to-mid 0.98-0.99 range.

#### Experiment 3: R2 vs. train/test split size

Switching to a regression setting (the Diabetes dataset, predicting disease progression from patient measurements) with plain `LinearRegression`:

| test_size | n_test | Train R2 | Test R2 |
|-----------|--------|----------|---------|
| 0.1 | 45 | 0.5302 | 0.3572 |
| 0.2 | 89 | 0.5539 | 0.3322 |
| 0.3 | 133 | 0.5539 | 0.3929 |
| 0.5 | 221 | 0.5731 | 0.4377 |

Train R2 stays fairly flat (around 0.53-0.57) regardless of split size, but test R2 fluctuates more (0.33-0.44), again reflecting that a smaller held-out set gives a noisier estimate of true generalisation performance.

#### Experiment 4: R2 vs. model complexity (polynomial degree)

Fixing an 80/20 split and increasing the degree of polynomial features fed into `LinearRegression`:

| degree | Train R2 | Test R2 |
|--------|----------|---------|
| 1 | 0.5539 | 0.3322 |
| 2 | 0.6320 | 0.2907 |
| 3 | 0.8870 | -5.6270 |

This is the clearest result in the whole set of experiments. Train R2 rises steadily as the model is given more flexibility (more polynomial terms to fit the training data), reaching 0.887 at degree 3, but test R2 collapses to **-5.63**, far worse than a model that simply predicted the mean every time (which would score 0). This is textbook overfitting: a degree-3 polynomial on a small, 10-feature dataset has enough capacity to fit noise in the training data that has no relationship to the test data at all.

#### Experiment 5: R2 vs. Ridge regularization strength (alpha), at degree 2

Holding the degree-2 polynomial features fixed and adding `Ridge` regularization:

| alpha | Train R2 | Test R2 |
|-------|----------|---------|
| 0.0 (no ridge) | 0.6320 | 0.2907 |
| 0.1 | 0.5524 | 0.3463 |
| 1.0 | 0.4658 | 0.3412 |
| 10.0 | 0.1765 | 0.1411 |
| 100.0 | 0.0246 | 0.0188 |

A small amount of regularization (`alpha = 0.1`) actually improves test R2 over the unregularized degree-2 model, by shrinking the coefficients that were fitting noise. But pushed too far (`alpha = 10` or `100`), the model becomes too constrained to fit the real signal either, and both train and test R2 collapse toward 0. This demonstrates that regularization strength is itself a parameter with a sweet spot, not something to maximise or minimise blindly.

### Reflection on the learning outcome

These experiments make a professional and ethical point concrete: AUC and R2 are not fixed properties of a dataset, they are properties of a specific model, trained with specific parameters, evaluated on a specific split. The same Breast Cancer data produced an AUC of 0.9946 when evaluated on training data, and a more honest 0.97-0.99 range with a proper test split, a difference that could mislead a clinical stakeholder if the methodological detail (same-data evaluation) were omitted from a report. Similarly, the polynomial regression experiment shows a model that looks excellent by one number (train R2 = 0.887) and catastrophic by the number that actually matters for deployment (test R2 = -5.63).

This has direct legal and professional implications. A practitioner who reports only a training-set metric, only a headline AUC without disclosing test set size or regularization choices, or only the metric that makes a model look best, is not lying outright, but is making a choice about what to disclose that can be professionally misleading, particularly in regulated domains like healthcare or finance where a model's reported performance can influence real decisions about patients or customers. Full, honest model evaluation requires disclosing the train/test methodology, the parameters tried, and the metric's sensitivity to small changes in test set size, not just the single best-looking number.

## Assessment 3: Individual Project, Recommender Systems (Track B)

This individual project was submitted directly via the university's assignment portal, not through this repository.

**Title:** Comparative Intelligence and Ethics in Recommender Systems: Classical SVD-GBDT vs Deep Hybrid NeuMF on MovieLens 1M

The brief for Track B asked for a comparison of a classical and a deep learning recommender system, evaluated on more than just accuracy: robustness, explainability, fairness and deployment cost. The scenario framing was a film platform choosing between two models to rank unseen films for its users.

### Dataset and preprocessing

The project used MovieLens 1M (Harper and Konstan, 2015): around one million ratings from 6,040 users across 3,706 films, chosen over the smaller 100K version because it gives small demographic subgroups (such as women over 56) enough data for meaningful confidence intervals, and because it includes gender, age band and occupation, needed for the later bias analysis. Over 95% of the user-film matrix is empty, and the top 20% of films account for roughly two-thirds of all ratings, a popularity imbalance that any model trained on this data inevitably inherits.

Preprocessing was kept minimal and leakage-safe: IDs were encoded, release year and an 18-dimension genre vector were derived, and ZIP code was deliberately dropped, since combined with gender and age it is highly re-identifying (Sweeney, 2000), consistent with the GDPR's data-minimisation principle (European Union, 2016, Art. 5(1)(c)). User and film average-rating features were computed out-of-fold, so a training rating never contributes to its own feature value, preventing gradient-boosted trees from learning from a leak that would otherwise inflate validation scores.

### Models and validation

Two models were compared. **SVD-GBDT** (scikit-learn; Pedregosa et al., 2011) factorises the residual rating matrix with TruncatedSVD to produce a collaborative score, then combines it with shrunken user/film averages, genre affinity and demographics in a histogram gradient-boosted tree ensemble, chosen because tree ensembles remain the strongest classical learners on tabular data (Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux, 2022) and support exact SHAP explanations. **Hybrid NeuMF** (He et al., 2017; implemented in PyTorch, Paszke et al., 2019) adapts Neural Collaborative Filtering to explicit ratings, keeping both the GMF dot-product branch and bias terms alongside the MLP tower with side information, a deliberate design choice responding to Rendle et al.'s (2020) finding that a plain dot product often beats NCF's MLP alone, so that any advantage from the deep model reflects genuine value from fusing side information rather than an unfair baseline comparison.

Validation used 5-fold cross-validation with every rating tested exactly once; within each training fold, 10% was held out for tuning and early stopping, keeping the test fold fully untouched by any modelling decision, since tuning on test data biases performance estimates optimistically (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). A noted limitation: the random split allows a model to see some of a user's future behaviour; a temporal split would be needed to confirm the results before any real deployment.

### Results

Both models comfortably beat a popularity-based baseline (RMSE 0.908), but differed from each other by only a few thousandths of a star: RMSE 0.854 for SVD-GBDT versus 0.857 for Hybrid NeuMF, and NDCG@10 of 0.840 versus 0.839. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test over per-user errors found no significant difference (p ~ 0.24), with the classical model ahead for 52% of users and the neural model for 48%, pointing to practical equivalence on accuracy, consistent with Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach's (2019) finding that many neural recommenders fail to beat well-tuned simpler methods.

Ranking the full catalogue (without negative sampling, following Krichene and Rendle, 2020) revealed a sharper trade-off than the rating-level metrics did: a simple most-popular list achieved the best NDCG@10 (0.168), but covered only 1.4% of the catalogue and recommended no long-tail films at all. Among the learned models, NeuMF had slightly better top-10 accuracy, while SVD-GBDT covered more of the catalogue (10.6% vs 7.6%) and surfaced more long-tail titles, the accuracy-diversity trade-off described by Kaminskas and Bridge (2016).

### Bias, fairness and privacy

Subgroup analysis (using bootstrap confidence intervals resampling users) found the least active fifth of users had an RMSE around 0.90 against 0.83 for the most active, women were served worse than men (0.888 vs 0.843, consistent with Ekstrand et al., 2018), and teenagers had the highest error of any age band. Both models also amplified popularity in their recommendations relative to what users actually rated, an effect nearly doubling for niche users under NeuMF (Abdollahpouri et al., 2019).

A privacy probe found that although the SVD factors never saw gender directly, a simple classifier recovered gender from them with an AUC of 0.70 (versus 0.55 from activity alone), demonstrating that removing a protected attribute from the input does not remove its signal from the learned representation (Weinsberg et al., 2012). This, combined with the re-identification risk of rating histories (Narayanan and Shmatikov, 2008), was framed against the GDPR's lawful-basis and impact-assessment requirements and the EU Digital Services Act's transparency obligations for large platforms (European Union, 2022, Arts 27 and 38).

### Explainability

TreeSHAP gave exact Shapley values for SVD-GBDT, summing to the prediction to fourteen decimal places, with a counter-intuitive dependence pattern: a high user average received a *negative* SHAP value, because the baseline feature already carried the user's generosity and the trees used the average as a correction, a known reading difficulty with correlated features in SHAP (Kumar et al., 2020). Comparing both models in the same seven-field input space showed NeuMF placing roughly double the weight on release decade and genre compared to SVD-GBDT, indicating it leans more on content signals. Captum's Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan, Taly and Yan, 2017) on NeuMF's embeddings agreed with SHAP at rank correlations of 0.86-0.89, cross-validating that the explanations reflected the model rather than an artefact of either method.

### Deployment recommendation

The project recommended deploying SVD-GBDT: equivalent accuracy, roughly half the training time, exact and auditable explanations supporting Digital Services Act transparency duties and the case for inherently interpretable models (Rudin, 2019), better catalogue coverage, and less popularity amplification. Its main weakness, higher per-user scoring latency (~21ms vs ~2ms), was addressed with a two-stage retrieve-then-rerank architecture (Covington, Adams and Sargin, 2016), alongside model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) and subgroup-aware monitoring of RMSE, NDCG, coverage, popularity lift and data drift (Gama et al., 2014) to catch any regression for a specific group before it reaches users.
