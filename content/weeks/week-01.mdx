## Initial Post

_by Lana Menezes Fidelis, Friday, 31 July 2026, 3:39 PM_

Metcalf (2024) makes the point that Industry 5.0 isn't really a clean break from Industry 4.0, but more of a course-correction: a recognition that connecting everything digitally brings real risks alongside the efficiency gains, and that human and societal impact needs to sit alongside the technology. Healthcare is a sector where that tension really shows.

A good example is the June 2024 ransomware attack on Synnovis, the pathology services provider for Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trusts in London. Attackers exploited a service account without multi-factor authentication, moved through the network, and encrypted systems, stopping blood test processing and transfusion matching almost overnight (NHS England, 2024). Over 800 planned operations and thousands of outpatient appointments were cancelled, and the disruption ran for months, with some patients even facing delayed cancer diagnoses because samples couldn't be processed.

The economic cost was significant, but arguably the reputational and patient-trust damage was worse: nearly 400GB of patient data, including NHS numbers and test results, was exfiltrated and later published when the ransom wasn't paid. It's a stark illustration of what Ghobakhloo et al. (2024) describe as the tension between Industry 4.0's push for interconnected efficiency and the resilience and human-centricity that Industry 5.0 is meant to prioritise. Khallaf et al. (2025) note that healthcare's rapid adoption of IoT and cyber-physical systems has outpaced the cybersecurity maturity needed to protect them, and Synnovis is a textbook case: a single weak credential brought an entire regional pathology network to a standstill.
